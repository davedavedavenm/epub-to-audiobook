In nineteen-seventeen Shin Fane — originally a non-violent, non-republican nationalist party — was reorganized and committed itself to an Irish republic. In the post-Rising period this party harvested most of what had been sown in nineteen-sixteen, and by the time of the UK general election in December nineteen-eighteen Shin Fane was set to triumph within nationalist Ireland. Although it won under half of the total vote, the party nevertheless gained seventy-three seats to the IPP’s dismal six and the twenty-six won by unionists. This was a resounding success for the party claiming inheritance to the nineteen-sixteen legacy and, following their victory, Shin Fane set up an alternative parliament in Dublin — Doyle Air-un — which comprised those Shin Faners elected in nineteen-eighteen and not imprisoned. This First Doyle became, for republicans, the truly legitimate authority in Ireland.

A kind of rebel government was formed, with the Doyle choosing a cabinet which included leading military men such as Michael Collins, CA-hal BROO-ha, and Richard Mulcahy — men who would play a major role in leading the IRA’s nineteen-nineteen to twenty-one war against the British. Shin Fane’s rebel government was in part political propaganda. It was far from being a fully functioning government, but it did represent a striking way of questioning British legitimacy in Ireland. If such a rival parliament could be elected, renouncing British rule, then where did that leave British legitimacy? Irish republicans were trying to produce a kind of republic within the old British order, and they fiercely proclaimed the superior legitimacy of their post-nineteen-eighteen regime.
